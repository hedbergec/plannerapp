Shadish\, Cook\, and Campbell \(2002\) define:

# Potential outcomes

# What is our goal?

In an evaluation\, our goal is to understand if an intervention __causes__ a different outcome than what would have occurred without otherwise\.

Embedded in this:

Intervention = a treatment\, program\, training\, etc\.

Outcome = any measure

Comparison = what would have occurred otherwise\, often ‘business\-as\-usual’

Causal effects are inherently \*comparative\*\.

# Internal Validity

# Internal validity refers to the extent to which a research design establishes a trustworthy cause-and-effect relationship between an intervention and an outcome.

# 7 common threats

In order to determine causality\, we need to rule out 7 threats:

Ambiguous temporal precedence

Maturation

Selection

Attrition

Testing effects

Instrumentation

Regression

# Ambiguous temporal precedence

Cause must precede effect\.

\(It’s not obvious that this is true in \*cross\-sectional\* designs\.\)

# Maturation

Over time\, individuals grow older\,

wiser and more experienced for

reasons that have nothing to do with interventions\.

\(It’s possible you would have eventually learned research design without us\.\)

# Selection

Groups taking part \(or not\) in

interventions often differ in a

variety of ways other than the

use of the intervention\.

\(Not everyone wants to spend a week learning research design\.\)

# Attrition

Often\, not every participant that begins

in a study persists throughout the

study\. Quitting may be related to

both the intervention and the outcome\.

\(Maybe we’re really boring? Maybe you already know this material?\)

# Testing effects

The act of observing \(or measuring\)

may change the phenomenon being

observed in substantial ways\.

\(Now that I’ve told you that your knowledge will be measured\, might you pay attention more?\)

# Instrumentation

The same instrument or observation

protocol might not measure the

same construct at different points in time\.

\(Might you think you know more about research design before you learn about research design? \*Dunning\-Kruger\* effect?\)

# Regression

If participants are selected into the study

because they are above or below their

group mean on this pretest\, then we would

expect their posttest scores to be closer to the mean\.

\(Why? Because the pre\-test knowledge is not perfectly measured – there is error in it\. This leads to ‘regression to the mean\.’\)

# Research designs rule these out

A research design that has \*strong internal validity\* rules these threats out by \*design\*\.

Weaker designs rule these out by \*assumption\*\.

The weakest designs don’t even rule them out\.

# External Validity (Generalizability)

# External validity refers to the issue of whether the relation observed between ‘cause’ and ‘effect’ can be generalized from the settings, persons and contexts studied to those in a broader population intended by the researcher.

# Return to potential outcomes

A research design that has \*strong external validity\* connects the sample to the population via a strong \*design\*\.

Weaker designs make this link using models and \*assumptions\*\.

The weakest designs don’t even acknowledge that they differ\.

# Generalizability isn’t generalizable

The generalizability of a study’s results is an ambiguous concept without specifying the __target of generalization__ —the supposed population or scope of applicability to which the study’s results are supposed to apply\.

For example\, the target population could be:

Education researchers interested in colleges of education

NSF funded education researchers

And so on…\.

# Initial field studies

It is \*very hard\* to conduct an evaluation that has good internal validity\.

It is even \*harder\* to conduct a study with both strong internal and external validity\.

For this reason\, we think that initial field studies should worry less about external validity than internal validity\.

Our position is that initial field studies should worry the most about having strong internal validity and save questions of external validity for efficacy and effectiveness evaluations\.

In practice\, this means treating the sample of participants / schools as ’fixed’ \(instead of as a ‘random’ sample\)\.

This choice and its implications should be made very clear in all research reports\.

# Statistical Conclusion Validity

# 3 threats to worry about

Unreliable measures

Incorrect data elements

Incorrect analyses

# Unreliable measures

A measure is reliability if it would give the

same result if collected again\.

\(Check those Cronbach alphas …\)

# Incorrect data elements

In quantitative studies\, this can happen

when data is coded incorrectly

\(e\.g\.\, there are errors in tabulation\,

matching\, or aggregation\)\.

\(This is where using careful\, reproducible code can help\, as can double checking any ‘surprising’ findings\.\)

# Incorrect analyses

There must be alignment between the

research design chosen and the statistical

analysis conducted\.

\(For example\, an ANOVA only results in the correct treatment effect estimate if the groups were randomized\.\)

# Construct Validity

# The final validity threat concerns whether the explanation given by the study has correctly identified the constructs in the interpretation.

# Examples of problems

Hawthorne effects

Novelty effects

# Keep in mind

Construct validity of explanation is __not__ a property of designs\, but of interpretation of evidence from designs\, which inevitably involve the __theoretical framework__ in which the research is embedded\.

While construct validity of explanation is crucial in the broader interpretation of evaluation research\, __it is difficult for a single evaluation study__ \, particularly a small\-scale study conducted in the development process\, to address it\.

# Conclusion

# Developing a strong research design involves careful consideration of a variety of potential threats.
